Three converging lines of research are quietly reshaping how large language models learn and reason: the discovery that *scaling compute at inference time* can rival gains from pretraining, the demonstration that *reinforcement learning alone* can teach models to think step-by-step, and a new family of memory-efficient optimizers that make training frontier-scale models accessible without industrial-scale hardware. Together, they mark a genuine shift in how the field thinks about intelligence in machines.

---

## Section 1: Key Research Papers

### 1. Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally Improves Language Model Reasoning
**Snell, Lee, Xu, Kumar — Google DeepMind | [arXiv:2408.03314](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03314)**

This paper asks a question that sounds almost too simple: what if instead of training a bigger model, you just let a smaller model *think longer*? The answer turns out to be non-trivial. Snell et al. show that the right approach to test-time scaling—using process reward models (PRMs) to guide beam search over intermediate reasoning steps—can enable a smaller, compute-constrained model to outperform a much larger model given the same inference budget.

The key insight is the distinction between *outcome reward models* (ORM, which score only the final answer) and *process reward models* (PRM, which evaluate every step in a chain-of-thought). PRMs make it possible to search productively through reasoning chains, discarding dead-ends early. The paper empirically shows that the optimal allocation of test-time compute is highly problem-dependent: easy questions benefit from simple majority voting across samples, while hard questions require the deeper search that PRMs enable.

What makes this consequential isn't just the accuracy numbers—it's what it implies for the scaling law narrative. Pretraining compute has dominated the conversation since GPT-3. This work suggests a complementary dimension: **inference-time compute**, and it's tractable today with existing open-weight models.

---

### 2. DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning
**DeepSeek-AI | [arXiv:2501.12948](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948)**

DeepSeek-R1 is one of the most technically interesting LLM papers in recent memory, and arguably the most democratizing. The central result: a base language model can be trained *entirely via reinforcement learning*—without any supervised chain-of-thought demonstrations—to develop long-form reasoning behaviors, including self-verification, backtracking, and what the authors call **"aha moments,"** where the model spontaneously reconsiders an incorrect intermediate step.

The training algorithm is Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), a simplified variant of PPO that removes the value network by computing baseline rewards from a group of sampled outputs rather than a learned critic. This is more memory-efficient and empirically stable for long-horizon reasoning tasks.

One of the more surprising findings: the model was observed to spontaneously allocate more tokens to difficult problems and fewer to easy ones—a form of adaptive computation that was never explicitly trained.

### 3. GaLore: Memory-Efficient LLM Training by Gradient Low-Rank Projection
**Zhao, Zhang, Chen, Wang, Anandkumar, Tian | [arXiv:2403.03507](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03507)**

LoRA and its variants have become the default approach to efficient fine-tuning, but they apply low-rank constraints to the *weight updates*, which limits their expressiveness and doesn't help when you need to train a model from scratch. GaLore takes a different angle: instead of constraining the weights, it constrains the *gradients*.

The key observation is that gradient matrices in LLMs tend to lie in a low-rank subspace that changes slowly over training. GaLore projects gradients onto this subspace before updating the optimizer state (Adam moments), which means Adam's second-moment accumulator—usually the memory bottleneck—only needs to track the low-rank projection rather than the full weight matrix. The result is a 65.5% reduction in optimizer memory with negligible impact on convergence.

Crucially, GaLore is optimizer-agnostic and works on full training runs, not just fine-tuning. The authors pre-trained LLaMA-7B from scratch on C4 using a single 24GB GPU, a task that would normally require tens of A100s.

## Section 2: Emerging Technical Themes

### The Convergence of Training-Time and Inference-Time Scaling

The most striking pattern across these papers is a **decomposition of intelligence into two computable resources**: the capacity baked in during training, and the capacity exercised during inference. For years, the dominant scaling paradigm was about training: more parameters, more tokens, more FLOPs. What's shifting now is the recognition that inference-time compute is also a scalable resource—and that the optimal strategy for allocating it varies dramatically depending on the problem structure.

This is not just a theoretical observation. Products like OpenAI's o-series and DeepSeek-R1 are deployed with explicit inference-time reasoning budgets. Research teams are designing evaluation benchmarks specifically for "thinking" models. The PRM literature has exploded. And the key open question—how to train PRMs at scale without human annotation of every intermediate step—is generating new lines of work in automatic step verification and synthetic data generation.

### RL as a First-Class Training Paradigm for Reasoning

DeepSeek-R1's success with GRPO has opened a door that was cracked by RLHF but not yet fully entered: using reinforcement learning not just for preference alignment, but as the *primary mechanism* for teaching reasoning. The implication is significant. If RL alone can produce strong reasoning chains—without thousands of hours of human annotators writing chain-of-thought examples—then the bottleneck for building reasoning models shifts from data collection to reward design and training stability.

This connects directly to the broader theme of **scalable oversight**: how do we specify what "good reasoning" means in a way that an RL training signal can faithfully optimize? As capabilities grow, this becomes increasingly non-trivial and is one of the central challenges in AI alignment today.

### Memory Efficiency as a Democratization Vector

GaLore and techniques like it matter not just for GPU cost reasons, but for the research ecosystem. When a 7B-parameter pretraining run fits on a single 24GB consumer GPU, it moves the frontier of what's experimentally tractable from industrial labs to university research groups and well-resourced individual contributors. That changes who gets to explore fundamental questions about training dynamics, architectural choices, and emergent behaviors—and it tends to accelerate the rate of discovery.

## Section 3: Deep Dive — DeepSeek-R1 and the Emergence of Reasoning from RL

### Background

When DeepSeek released R1 in January 2025, it sparked an immediate debate in the research community: was this a genuine breakthrough in understanding how reasoning emerges, or an engineering achievement dressed up as science? After reading the paper carefully, the answer is—genuinely both.

The setup is deceptively straightforward. Start with DeepSeek-V3 as the base model. Apply RL using GRPO with a simple rule-based reward: full credit for a correct final answer in a verifiable domain (math competition problems, coding tasks), zero for incorrect answers. No human-labeled reasoning chains. No process-level reward signal. No CoT demonstrations.

### What Happens During Training

What the DeepSeek team observed during training is the kind of result that makes researchers stop and reread the paper. At some point in the RL training loop, the model begins producing responses with a distinctive structure: it generates an initial answer, then issues something like *"Wait, let me reconsider"*—unprompted—and revises its approach. The authors call these "aha moments."

These moments weren't programmed in. They emerged from gradient updates optimizing for final-answer correctness. The model discovered that reconsidering was instrumentally useful, and generalized this to an explicit behavior.

### The GRPO Mechanism

Standard PPO for LLMs requires maintaining both a policy network and a value network, doubling the memory footprint and introducing a separate training loop for the value function. GRPO sidesteps this by replacing the learned value baseline with a group-level baseline: for each prompt, sample *G* outputs, compute rewards for each, and normalize by the group mean and standard deviation. The policy gradient signal is then the advantage relative to the group—which, in expectation, approximates the PPO advantage without needing a learned critic.

For long reasoning chains, this is particularly important because the variance in rollout rewards is high. The group normalization provides a stable training signal even when most sampled outputs fail to reach the correct answer.

### Two-Stage Training: R1-Zero vs. R1

The paper distinguishes two systems. **R1-Zero** is the pure RL result described above—it demonstrates that reasoning can emerge from RL alone, but it has readability issues (the model sometimes mixes languages mid-reasoning, produces verbose redundancy, or uses unfamiliar notation).

**R1** addresses these issues with a cold-start phase: a small set (a few thousand examples) of carefully formatted reasoning chains bootstrapped from R1-Zero are used for supervised fine-tuning before RL training begins. This shapes the output format without suppressing the underlying reasoning capability. The result is a model that reasons as effectively as R1-Zero but produces reasoning chains that humans and downstream tools can actually follow.

### What This Means for AI Alignment

This is where the paper gets philosophically interesting. The "aha moment" behavior—self-correction without explicit training for it—is a clean empirical demonstration of **instrumental convergence**: the model learned a behavior (reconsideration) because it happened to be useful for the objective it was trained on, not because it was specified. This is the dynamic that AI alignment researchers have been modeling theoretically for years, now visible in training logs.

It doesn't make R1 dangerous or misaligned—the behavior here is benign and useful. But it is a concrete demonstration of the mechanism that becomes concerning at higher capability levels and with less transparent objectives. Understanding when and why these emergent behaviors appear is now an empirical question, and GRPO-style training provides a tractable experimental setup for studying it.

### Benchmarks and Results

On AIME 2024 (American Invitational Mathematics Examination), DeepSeek-R1 achieves 79.8% pass@1, compared to OpenAI o1's 79.2%. On Codeforces problems, it reaches a rating of 2029, placing it in the 96.3rd percentile among human participants. These are not marginal improvements—they represent genuine problem-solving capability on tasks that require sustained, multi-step reasoning.

The distilled versions (R1 reasoning capabilities transferred into smaller models via knowledge distillation) are particularly interesting for practitioners: a 7B distilled model outperforms the base DeepSeek-V3 67B on several math benchmarks, which has significant implications for deployment cost.

## Section 4: Open Questions & Expert Commentary

### Can Test-Time Scaling Generalize Beyond Math and Code?

The current success of PRM-guided search and extended reasoning chains is concentrated in domains with verifiable answers: mathematics, competitive programming, formal logic. These domains allow unambiguous reward signals, which makes RL training and search tractable.

The harder question is whether these techniques transfer to open-ended tasks—creative writing, strategic reasoning under uncertainty, scientific hypothesis generation—where the correct answer isn't checkable by a rule. Some preliminary work on **Constitutional AI** and **debate-based oversight** (Anthropic, Irving et al.) points toward approaches, but none have matched the effectiveness seen in verifiable domains. This remains one of the most important unsolved problems in the field.

### How Do We Build Better Process Reward Models?

PRMs are a bottleneck for test-time scaling. Human-labeled reasoning step quality is expensive and slow to collect. The most promising directions involve synthetic data—using a stronger model to label the steps of a weaker model, or using formal verifiers to check intermediate states in structured domains. But the gap between PRM quality on math problems and PRM quality on messy real-world tasks remains large, and there's no clear scaling path to close it.

### What Happens to the Reasoning-Capability Gap as Models Are Distilled?

R1's distilled 7B model is impressive, but there's a known pattern in knowledge distillation where certain behaviors that emerge from scale don't survive compression. The "aha moments" and extended self-verification chains may be behaviors that require a certain parameter count to sustain reliably. This matters for deployment: if reasoning robustness degrades sharply below a parameter threshold, it limits the hardware tiers where capable reasoning models are viable.

### The Memory Efficiency Frontier

GaLore is a first step toward memory-efficient full training, but the gradient subspace assumption—that gradients lie in a slowly-changing low-rank structure—may not hold across all architectures and training phases. Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, for instance, have very different gradient dynamics due to the discrete routing mechanism. How these techniques scale to MoE architectures, which are now the dominant paradigm for large models, is an open question.

## Call-to-Think

The "aha moment" in DeepSeek-R1 raises a question worth sitting with: if a model can spontaneously develop a useful behavior—self-correction—without being explicitly trained for it, **what other behaviors might be emerging in large models that we haven't yet named or noticed?** And how would we recognize them if they were less benign?

Process reward models and extended reasoning chains give us a window into a model's intermediate steps. That interpretability may turn out to be as important as the performance gains.
